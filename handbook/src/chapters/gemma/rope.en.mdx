# RoPE: position by rotation

<p className="lead">Attention gives tokens no <b>order</b>: a shuffled sequence produces the same scores. To know positions, information has to enter from somewhere. Gemma does not add anything to the embedding. It <b>rotates</b> the Q and K vectors according to their positions.</p>

## The idea: let position be an angle

In SigLIP, position was a learned vector from a table that was <i>added</i> to the patch embeddings (<Link to="/patches">2.2</Link>). RoPE (rotary position embedding) works differently: it splits the <code>head_dim</code> vector of Q and K into pairs and rotates each pair in its own 2D plane by **position × θ**.

$$
\begin{pmatrix} a' \\ b' \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} \cos m\theta & -\sin m\theta \\ \sin m\theta & \cos m\theta \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix}
$$

Below is a single pair: <i>q</i> at position m, <i>k</i> at position n. Play with the sliders. Then press "shift both +1" and watch the dot product.

<Fig wide caption={<><b>One pair rotating.</b> Every pair has its own speed θ: pair 0 turns 1 radian per position, pair 100 about 0.0007 radians. The arrows rotate, but the angle between them, and therefore <span className="mono">q · k</span>, only depends on <span className="mono">m − n</span>.</>}>
<RopeDial />
</Fig>

The reason is that the two rotations partly cancel each other:

$$
(R_{m\theta}\, q)\cdot(R_{n\theta}\, k) \;=\; q^\top R_{m\theta}^\top R_{n\theta}\, k \;=\; q^\top R_{(n-m)\theta}\, k
$$

The score depends on the **difference** of the positions, not on the positions themselves. "Three tokens back" means the same thing wherever it happens in the sequence.

## 128 pairs, 128 speeds

`head_dim = 256`, so 128 pairs. Pair <span className="mono">i</span> turns at <span className="mono">θᵢ = 10000^(−2i/256)</span>: the first pairs are fast, the last ones very slow. The frequencies below come from the project's `GemmaRotaryEmbedding` class.

<Fig caption={<><b>Fast pairs separate nearby positions, slow pairs distant ones.</b> Pair 0 completes a turn about every 6 tokens. Pair 127 takes more than 50 thousand tokens. Like the hands of a clock: the second hand shows the recent past, the hour hand the distant past.</>}>
<RopeFreqs />
</Fig>

<Fig caption={<><b>How the score depends on the position difference.</b> q and k have equal length and start in the same direction (the best case). Fast pairs die out quickly, slow pairs carry information over long distances. Below, measured with the project's code: four different position pairs with a difference of 5 give <b>the same score</b>.</>}>

## Which dims form a pair?

In the paper the pairs are neighboring dims: <span className="mono">(0, 1), (2, 3), …</span>. In HF and in this project's code the pairs are <b>first half with second half</b>: <span className="mono">(0, 128), (1, 129), …</span>. Both describe the same rotation; they only change the order in which the dims are laid out. But Gemma's weights were trained for the <b>half/half layout</b>. Pick the wrong layout and nothing raises; the result just breaks silently.

<Fig wide caption={<><b>The same rotation, two layouts.</b> If you reorder the dims (adjacent pair → half/half), the two methods give exactly the same result. If you apply both to the raw vector without reordering, different pairs rotate and the results differ. Measured with the project's <code>apply_rotary_pos_emb</code>.</>}>

</Fig>

## Positions in bf16

The model runs in bf16, but the angles are computed in float32. The reason: bf16 has 7 mantissa bits, so it cannot represent large integers exactly.

<Fig caption={<><b>What happens to a position converted to bf16?</b> Up to 256 every position is exact. After that the step doubles with every doubling: steps of 2 after 256, steps of 32 after 4096. Pair 0 turns 1 radian per position, so every unit of error in the position is one radian of error in the angle.</>}>
<Bf16Angle />
</Fig>

<Callout kind="dikkat" title="Keep float32">
That is why the line <code>position_ids[..., None].float()</code> is there. Only the angle is in float32. After cos and sin are computed they go back to the dtype of Q/K (the <code>.to(x.dtype)</code> on the last line).
</Callout>

## Concept questions

<ConceptQ n={1} q="Why is RoPE applied to Q and K instead of being added to the embedding? Why not to V?">
Position has to show up in the <b>score</b>, and the score is <span className="mono">q · k</span>. A position added to the embedding gets mixed with other information over the layers, fades, and has to be reminded in every layer. With Q and K rotated, position enters the score directly in every attention of every layer. <b>V</b> describes "what it carries": it is mixed as content in the weighted sum. Rotating it would distort the output according to the position of the token it looked at.
</ConceptQ>

<ConceptQ n={2} q="Why does the score between two tokens depend only on their position difference?">
Because <span className="mono">R(mθ)ᵀ R(nθ) = R((n − m)θ)</span>: the product of the two rotations is the rotation of the difference. That is why the dot product does not change when you shift both sliders together above.
</ConceptQ>

<Predict q="The KV cache stores a token's K. Should K be stored before or after RoPE?" hint="When token 260 is generated, are the earlier tokens' K's recomputed?">
<b>After</b>. The rotated K is stored, because each token is rotated once at its own position and never changes again. When a new token is generated, only its Q and K are rotated, with <b>its own position</b> (<code>position_ids</code> = 260), and the old K's are left alone. That is why <code>position_ids</code> is a separate argument of <code>GemmaModel</code>: the table does not always start at 0. See <Link to="/gemma/kv-cache">4.12</Link>.
</Predict>
